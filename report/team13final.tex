\tightsection{Motivations and Objectives}
Competitive Pokemon seems simple at first. Stronger Pokemon should have higher stats, and higher stats should lead to higher usage. We test this idea using VGC 2022 data.
Our main finding is that raw power is not the whole story. Team fit also matters. A Pokemon's usage is influenced by its teammates, role, moves, items, abilities, and team structure.
The goal of this project is to build a visualization that is easy for non-expert viewers to understand. Rather than creating a large Pokemon dashboard, we focus on a single question: Why are some Pokemon used so often?
Our story shows that base stats alone do not explain competitive usage. Team connectivity and role evidence help explain why Pokemon such as Incineroar remain highly valuable. The interface follows a Martini Glass narrative structure \cite{segel2010narrative}: Assumption, Contradiction, Network Reveal, and Explore.

\tightsection{Driving Application and Datasets}
The project focuses on the 2022 Video Game Championships (VGC) metagame. The main question is simple: \textit{What makes a Pokemon successful in competitive play?}
We use two public datasets. The main dataset contains Pokemon forms, base stats, usage rates, teammates, moves, items, and abilities \cite{carbone2022competitive}. The second dataset provides Pokemon images used throughout the interface \cite{singh32000pokemon}.
After preprocessing, the final dataset contains 1,066 Pokemon forms, 2,606 teammate links, and 2,967 build records. These data support the scatterplot, network visualization, ego comparison, role evidence, and guided exploration views used in the story.

\tightsection{Challenges to Address}
One challenge was deciding how much information to include. The dataset contains stats, teammates, moves, items, and abilities. One of the biggest changes from our original plan was reducing the number of views. Early designs explored separate analyses for Pokemon stats, team networks, competitive roles, and build information, as well as larger network visualizations with more Pokemon and teammate links. During implementation, we found that these designs felt more like a dashboard than a story and made the central argument harder to follow. We therefore focused on one question: if raw stats do not fully explain usage, what does? This led us to use a smaller set of coordinated views built around a single argument.

Another challenge was making the network readable. Large Pokemon networks can quickly become cluttered with many nodes and links. To reduce visual noise, we focused on important Pokemon and strong teammate relationships. Node size represents usage, edge width represents teammate co-usage strength, and interactive highlighting helps readers follow local network structure.

A third challenge was connecting multiple visualizations into one story. Scatterplots, correlation summaries, networks, and role evidence answer different questions. We addressed this with a Martini Glass narrative structure, where each view provides evidence for the next stage of the story before readers enter open exploration.

\tightsection{Background and Related Work}
The project follows principles from narrative visualization, especially Segel and Heer’s discussion of guided stories with later reader-driven exploration \cite{segel2010narrative}. This idea directly influenced our Martini Glass structure, where readers first follow an authored explanation before entering open exploration.

We also use coordinated multiple views. The scatterplot, signal scan, network, comparison view, picker, and detail panel share the same selected Pokemon state so that readers can follow one case study across the story instead of treating each chart independently.

D3 supports the custom visual encodings and interactions used throughout the visualizations \cite{d3js}. React manages application state and coordinated selections across views \cite{react}. Together, these technologies support the coordinated storytelling structure used throughout the project.

\tightsection{Design and Methodology}
The final design uses a Martini Glass structure because the project asks viewers to revise a common assumption about strength. The hero introduces the assumption that stronger Pokemon should be used more. The scatterplot tests that assumption, the signal scan shifts the explanation toward teammate connectivity, the network reveal visualizes repeated team structure, and guided exploration lets readers test the same thesis themselves. The StoryStepper keeps this sequence visible as Assumption, Contradiction, Network Reveal, and Explore.

Figures 1 and 2 establish the opening argument. The scatterplot shows the contradiction: total stats and usage only weakly align. The signal scan then compares candidate explanations and identifies teammate connectivity as the strongest observed signal.

The visual encodings are intentionally consistent. In the scatterplot, x-position is base stat total, y-position is usage percentage, color is primary type, and a red outline marks the selected Pokemon. In the signal scan, bar length is absolute correlation with usage. In the network, node size is usage, node color is primary type, and edge width is teammate co-usage strength. In the ego comparison, node size and edge width keep the same meanings, while shared and unique teammates are visually separated.

Together, these encodings allow readers to compare Pokemon consistently across views while preserving the narrative flow of the story.

\subsection*{Ego Comparison Design Rationale}
The full network is necessary for the reveal, but its density makes direct pairwise comparison difficult. The ego comparison exists to isolate two local teammate neighborhoods while still showing usage, total stats, connectivity degree, and teammate footprint. This makes the thesis concrete: a lower-stat Pokemon can maintain high usage when it appears in a broader and more reusable teammate ecosystem.

\subsection*{Guided Exploration}
We avoided a traditional dashboard because the final stage should extend the story, not reset it. Guided missions ask readers to find another Incineroar-like case, inspect a failed stat monster, compare support and attacker roles, and test how rankings change when connectivity becomes the focus. Each mission provides a question, compact evidence, a conclusion, and a next step; the ranked picker then supports open search while remaining tied to the thesis.

\tightsection{Implementation Considerations}
The implementation is a Vite React application that combines D3 visualizations with a coordinated storytelling interface. During data loading, the app reads cleaned CSV files, loads Pokemon image assets, computes network metrics, and builds a reduced teammate network from the co-usage dataset. React state stores the selected Pokemon, comparison Pokemon, brushed scatterplot selection, and active narrative step.

The main implementation decision was using shared selection across all views. The scatterplot, signal scan, network, ego comparison, picker, and detail panel all respond to the same selected Pokemon. This allows readers to follow a single case study throughout the story rather than treating each visualization as an independent chart. Brushing highlights related network nodes, while clicking a network node or picker result updates the comparison and detail views.

\subsection*{Narrative Coordination Challenges}
The largest implementation challenge was supporting a storytelling structure instead of building a traditional dashboard. The contradiction view and signal scan needed to function as one story stage, while the network, ego comparison, and evidence views needed to act as a connected explanation. The StoryStepper also had to stay synchronized with scrolling and direct navigation so the Martini Glass narrative remained visible throughout the experience.

We addressed this challenge by maintaining a shared selection model and a single active narrative state across the application. This ensured continuity between views: one selected Pokemon, one active story stage, and one argument carried across the entire page.

Another bottleneck was network readability. Early designs explored larger network displays with more Pokemon and teammate links, but these versions became visually cluttered and weakened the story. The final design filters the network toward case-study Pokemon and high-signal teammate relationships. Node labels are reserved for important Pokemon and archetypes, while hover and click interactions reveal additional detail only when needed.

We also used supplemental Pokemon sprite assets throughout the interface and report figures. These sprites improve recognition, reduce label scanning, and make dense network views easier to interpret. All figures included in the report are screenshots from the implemented application rather than mockups, ensuring that the report documents the actual system that was built.

\tightsection{Evaluation Methods, Case Studies, and Insights}
We evaluated the project through case-study analysis and task-based design checks. The main evaluation question was whether a reader could explain why raw stats alone are not enough to explain competitive usage after moving through the story. A successful reader should be able to follow the progression from the scatterplot contradiction, to the signal scan explanation, to the network reveal and ego comparison. We also evaluated whether shared selection allowed one Pokemon to be tracked consistently across all views.

The most important quantitative insight is the difference between raw stats and teammate connectivity. In the cleaned usage-tracked data, base stat total shows only a weak relationship with usage (r = 0.19), while connectivity degree shows a much stronger relationship (r = 0.90). This does not prove causation, but it supports the central thesis that team relationships explain competitive relevance better than raw power alone.

The Incineroar case study provides the clearest example. Incineroar has a base stat total of only 530, yet it maintains 59\% usage and one of the largest teammate footprints in the dataset. Its common abilities, moves, and teammates help explain this pattern. Intimidate, Fake Out, and Parting Shot create team value, while repeated partners such as Zacian and Amoonguss show how it fits into successful team structures.

Several comparison cases help validate the story. Zacian Crowned Sword represents a case where high stats and high usage align. In contrast, Zamazenta Crowned Shield and Mewtwo show that high raw stats do not automatically produce high usage. Together, these examples support the conclusion that competitive success depends on both individual strength and team fit.

\subsection*{Reader Tasks}

The guided missions also function as evaluation tasks. Readers are asked to find another Incineroar-like case, inspect a high-stat Pokemon with lower usage, compare support and attacker roles, and explore how rankings change when connectivity becomes the focus. These tasks help verify that the story can be understood and reproduced through interaction rather than simply read as a fixed explanation.

One limitation of the evaluation is that the project uses usage and co-usage data rather than direct battle outcomes. As a result, the conclusions focus on observed metagame demand and team structure rather than competitive performance alone.

\tightsection{Directions for Further Work}
With more time, we would extend the project in several directions. One part of the original plan that was not fully implemented was broader exploration of competitive team structures. Early designs considered larger network views, additional role-analysis visualizations, and comparisons across multiple metagames, but these ideas were reduced so the final story could remain focused and readable.

We would also add win-rate or tournament-result data so usage could be compared against direct competitive performance. Another extension would be to analyze multiple seasons and metagames to study how team ecosystems change over time. Automatic role discovery could help identify common team patterns without manual case selection.

Finally, we would conduct a formal user study with both novice and experienced Pokemon players. This would help evaluate whether the Martini Glass narrative structure improves understanding compared with a traditional exploratory dashboard.
